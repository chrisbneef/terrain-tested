## What is viscosity?

Viscosity is an oil's resistance to flow. Thick oil has high viscosity and thin oil has low viscosity. It is the single most important property of a lubricant, because the oil film has to be thick enough to keep metal parts apart and thin enough to reach them quickly.

Every oil gets thinner as it heats up and thicker as it cools down. The grade on the bottle tells you how the oil behaves at both ends.

## How do you read a grade like 10W-40?

The grades come from a standard called SAE J300, published by SAE International. A multigrade oil such as 10W-40 carries two ratings:

- **The first number, with the W.** The W stands for winter. It rates how well the oil cranks and pumps at low temperature. The lower the number, the better the oil flows on a cold start. A 5W oil flows better in the cold than a 15W oil.
- **The second number.** This rates the oil's viscosity at 100°C (212°F), which is close to normal operating temperature. The higher the number, the thicker the oil stays when the engine is hot.

So a 10W-40 passes the cold-flow tests for a 10W oil and has the hot viscosity of an SAE 40 oil. It does not get thicker as it warms up. It simply thins out less than a straight-grade oil would.

## What about a single number, like SAE 30?

A straight-grade oil such as SAE 30 meets only the hot viscosity requirement. It has no cold-weather rating. Straight grades are still specified for some small air-cooled engines that run in warm weather.

## Which grades are common in which machines?

These are typical examples only. Your manual is the authority.

| Grade | Where it is commonly specified |
| --- | --- |
| 0W-20, 5W-30 | Modern passenger cars and light trucks |
| 10W-40, 20W-50 | Many motorcycles, ATVs and UTVs |
| 15W-40, 5W-40 | Diesel engines in trucks and heavy equipment |
| SAE 30, 10W-30 | Small four-stroke engines such as mowers and generators |

## Is thicker oil better protection?

No. The right viscosity is the one the engine was designed around. Oil that is too thick is slow to reach bearings on a cold start, which is when a large share of engine wear happens. Oil that is too thin may not hold a film under heat and load. Engine builders choose a grade to match bearing clearances, oil pump output and operating temperature.

## Where do synthetics fit in?

Viscosity grade and oil type are separate things. A synthetic 10W-40 and a conventional 10W-40 meet the same grade when new. The difference shows up in use: synthetic base oils generally flow better in the cold, resist heat better, and hold their grade longer under shear. That matters most in machines that run hot, rev high or work long hours.

## How do you choose?

1. Find the viscosity grade and the performance specification in your owner's or service manual.
2. Check whether the manual gives different grades for different temperature ranges.
3. Pick an oil that meets both the grade and the specification.
4. If your use is harder than normal, such as racing, towing or long hours under load, ask before you change grades.
