## What does an oil analysis report measure?

A standard report covers three things: what is wearing, what is getting in, and how much life the oil has left.

| Category | What the lab looks for | What it can point to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wear metals | Iron, copper, lead, aluminum, chromium | Wear in cylinders, bearings, bushings, pistons and rings |
| Contamination | Silicon, sodium, potassium, water, fuel, soot | Dirt getting past the air filter, coolant leaks, fuel dilution |
| Oil condition | Viscosity, oxidation, base number (TBN) | Whether the oil is still in grade and still has reserve additive left |

No single number tells the whole story. Silicon, for example, usually means dirt, but it can also come from sealants or from an additive in the oil itself. That is why the lab compares your results with typical values for the engine and, more importantly, with your own earlier samples.

## Why do trends matter more than one sample?

One report is a snapshot. A series of reports from the same machine, taken at consistent intervals, shows direction. Iron that climbs a little every sample is a different story from iron that doubles between two samples. The second one is a reason to look at the machine before it stops on its own.

## When is oil analysis worth doing?

- **To extend a drain interval.** The report shows whether the oil could have stayed in longer, so you can lengthen the interval in steps and confirm each one.
- **On expensive or hard-to-replace machines.** The cost of a sample is small next to an engine, a hydraulic pump or a final drive.
- **Before buying used equipment.** A sample can reveal coolant leaks or abnormal wear that a walk-around will not.
- **After a problem.** Overheating, a dusty job or a suspected coolant leak are all good reasons to check.
- **Across a fleet.** Sampling on a schedule finds the one machine that is behaving differently from the rest.

For a mower that gets one oil change a year, it is rarely necessary.

## How do you take a good sample?

1. Run the machine to operating temperature so contaminants are suspended in the oil.
2. Take the sample the same way every time: with a sample pump through the dipstick tube, from a sampling valve, or mid-stream from the drain.
3. Do not catch the first oil out of the drain plug or the last. The middle of the stream is most representative.
4. Use a clean sample bottle and keep dirt out of it.
5. Record the hours or miles on the machine and on the oil, plus any oil added since the last change.
6. Send it promptly.

## What do you do with the results?

Most labs flag each result as normal, worth watching or needing action, and add comments. Normal results mean keep going and sample again at the next interval. A flagged result usually means one of three things: shorten the interval, find and fix a source of contamination, or inspect a component.
